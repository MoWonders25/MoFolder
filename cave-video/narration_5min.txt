Imagine this. You're two thousand meters underground. That's deeper than five Empire State Buildings stacked on top of each other. Your rope just snapped. Your radio is dead. Rescue could take a month. Could you survive thirty days in the deepest cave on Earth? Stay till the end, because what happens on day thirty is something scientists actually tested, and the results were bizarre.

Welcome to Veryovkina Cave, in the mountains of Abkhazia, Georgia. It drops more than two thousand two hundred meters straight down. Explorers need about a week just to reach the bottom. And today, that's where you are. To put that in perspective, if you dropped a stone from the entrance, it would take minutes of bouncing and falling just to reach the floor. Up there, it's a sunny afternoon. Down here, the sun simply doesn't exist.

Day one. The first problem is darkness. Not night-time darkness. Total darkness. You switch off your headlamp to save the battery. It's about five degrees Celsius down here, and the air is soaking wet. Your clothes will never dry, so your biggest early danger is getting too cold. You sit on your backpack, off the cold rock, and wrap yourself in your emergency blanket. Wave your hand in front of your face, and you won't see a thing. Real cave explorers always carry three light sources. You have one headlamp and one spare battery, so every minute of light is precious.

Day three. Good news: water isn't a problem. Caves are full of it. Collect the drips, not the puddles. Food is another story. You break your energy bars into tiny pieces. A person can survive around three weeks without food, but every day you'll get weaker and colder. Quick question: what's the one item you'd bring into a cave? Write it in the comments. And here's a weird tip: don't eat everything at once. Digesting food uses up water and energy. Small snacks spread across the day keep your body warmer and steadier.

Day seven. Now your brain starts lying to you. Without sunlight, your body clock drifts. In 1962, French geologist Michel Siffre lived alone one hundred thirty meters underground in the French Alps, right next to an underground glacier, for sixty-three days. When his team called down to say the experiment was over, he thought it was still August and that he had a whole month to go. So you try a trick: every time you wake up, you drop one pebble into your pocket. Meanwhile, the silence is so deep that you can hear your own heartbeat. Some people even hear music that isn't there. That's your brain trying to fill the emptiness. So you give it a job. You sing songs, retell your favorite movie scene by scene, and plan your dream house.

Day fourteen. You switch on your light, and something moves. Relax. Cave creatures are tiny, and many have no eyes at all. Some cave fish can live for a hundred years and go months without eating. Honestly? They're better at this than you.

Day eighteen. Then you hear a rumble. Water. Rain on the surface can flood a cave within hours. You scramble up to the highest ledge you can find and look for old mud lines on the walls. They show how high the water has risen before. The water stops just below your boots. You hold your breath and wait for hours. Slowly, the water drains away, leaving the rock shiny and wet. That was close. Far too close.

Day twenty-five. You're pale, thinner, and even your weak headlamp feels blinding. Your mood swings up and down. But the people who survive are the ones who keep doing small tasks. So you keep stacking pebbles. Without sunlight, your body stops making vitamin D, and your muscles start to shrink. You keep drinking water, keep moving a little each day, and keep singing badly.

Day thirty. Voices. Lights far above you. The rescue team has arrived. They ask what day you think it is, and you proudly say day twenty-two. You're wrong by more than a week. Here's that bizarre result I promised: ten years later, Siffre spent six months in Midnight Cave in Texas, and his body slipped into a forty-eight-hour day. He stayed awake for about thirty-six hours, slept for twelve, and felt completely normal.

Rescuers cover your eyes against the sunlight, and doctors start you on small meals, not a feast. Eating a giant meal right after starving can actually be dangerous. Here's one last bonus fact: even the deepest cave on Earth goes only about 0.03 percent of the way to the center of the planet. We've barely scratched the surface. You made it.
